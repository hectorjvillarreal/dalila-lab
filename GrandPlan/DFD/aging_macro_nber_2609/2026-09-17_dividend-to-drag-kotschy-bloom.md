## Provenance Note

Different provenance from the six papers filed alongside it. Dated 31 March 2026, supersedes an
earlier working paper titled "Population Aging and Economic Growth: From Demographic Dividend to
Demographic Drag?", and does not follow the NBER conference filename convention of the others.
Filed as **related material** rather than as a seventh conference paper. Kotschy is NBER- and
Harvard Chan-affiliated; whether it appeared on the September program is not established here.

## Summary

Rainer Kotschy and David E. Bloom fit an empirical growth model to panel data for 145 countries
over 1950–2015, instrumenting growth of the working-age share with the lagged population share
aged 15–19 (the predetermined inflow into working ages), then feed projected age structures into
the fitted model to project income per capita over 2020–2050 against a counterfactual with age
structure frozen.

Core estimate: a 1% rise in the working-age share raises income per capita by about 1% through
an immediate growth effect and by 0.1–0.4% in subsequent periods through a level effect. Stable
across specifications, over time, and for both OECD and non-OECD samples.

Projection: **demographic drag will be the norm.** Age-structure shifts slow growth in 70 sample
countries — virtually all those whose working-age shares contract. The OECD average annual growth
rate slows by 0.8pp on chronological measures, 0.4pp on prospective ones. The authors' framing:
a demographic drag is the flipside of the demographic dividend, and will be the norm as countries
advance through the later stages of the demographic transition.

**This is the paper the six conference acquisitions collectively lacked: it poses the
dividend-to-drag question directly, globally, and from the author who named the dividend (Bloom
et al. 2003).**

Methodological note in DFD's favour: they use **income per capita, not per worker**, explicitly
to capture welfare per person rather than labour productivity. This removes the outcome-variable
objection recorded against Acemoglu, Autor, Beirne & Scott.

## The Contribution That Matters Most for DFD

The novelty is the distinction between **retrospective** (chronological) and **prospective** age
structure. Prospective measures, following Sanderson and Scherbov (2005, 2007, 2010, 2019), set
the old-age threshold by remaining life expectancy — the age at which expected remaining years
fall below fifteen (POAT) — which the authors show correlates at the macro level with functional
capacity in mortality, disability, body strength and cognition. Retrospective and prospective
measures then bound the effect of age-structure change under fixed versus evolving functional
capacity.

**The direction of the correction flips by transition phase:**

| Phase | Prospective vs retrospective | Magnitude |
|---|---|---|
| **Drag** (contracting working-age share) | Prospective gives a **smaller** drag | OECD 0.8pp → 0.4pp; Japan −0.89pp → −0.13pp; Chile −0.64 → −0.09; Sweden −0.45 → −0.06 |
| **Dividend** (expanding working-age share) | Prospective gives a **smaller and shorter** dividend | "The window of opportunity for a demographic dividend might be shorter than the retrospective measures of age structure suggest. The labor potential is smaller because average functional capacity is low." |

**Chronological measures flatter both ends: they overstate the drag facing old rich countries
and overstate the dividend remaining to younger, less healthy ones.** Up to half the OECD drag
could be compensated by expanded labour potential, but only where remaining life expectancy among
older people is comparatively high; where it is low (several Eastern and Southern European
countries, Germany, the Netherlands) only a smaller portion is compensable.

## DFD Calibration Implications

**Mexico.** Ranks second or third most favourable of thirty-four OECD countries in the 2020–2050
projection, across every specification (Figure 4 and Appendix B.5) — consistent with Kopecky and
with Benzell, Kotlikoff & Ye, and consistent with the 2050-horizon argument recorded in the
synthesis note.

But applying the paper's own mechanism to Mexico worsens the picture in **both** directions at
once:

1. **The remaining dividend is smaller than the chronological measure shows**, because Mexican
   functional capacity at older ages sits below the OECD leaders'. The prospective correction
   shrinks Mexico's remaining window.
2. **When the drag arrives, less of it is compensable**, for the same reason — the offset is
   largest exactly where remaining life expectancy at older ages is high.

Mexico is disadvantaged on both sides of a single parameter. **This is a sharper statement of
DFD's premise than anything in the six conference notes, and it is the paper's own mechanism
rather than an inference imposed on it.**

**Institutional condition, Mexico-specific.** The authors state that realising the prospective
offset depends on people's retirement preferences and on whether labour markets and health and
social policy can retain older workers productively. In Mexico older workers are already
disproportionately informal, so extending working life plausibly extends *informal* working life
— visible in GDP per capita and absent from the contributory base. This is the same
growth-versus-fiscal wedge running through the whole set, appearing here in a new form.
Original observation; requires Anne's assessment.

**Methodological question for the demographic module (Anne's authority).** Should the age
structure feeding IM-6 use chronological or prospective/functional thresholds?

Not rhetorical. NTA age profiles are chronological by construction, and DFD's methodology
principles hold NTA as the shared demographic language with departures requiring documentation
and justification. Adopting prospective measures would be exactly such a departure, with a real
cost in comparability against the wider NTA literature. But if Kotschy and Bloom are right,
chronological measures systematically mis-state age-structure effects in both phases, and a model
calibrated on them inherits the bias. **Defensible either way; posed as a question, not a
recommendation.**

**Fast-transition discipline.** Reinforces the discipline's direction while relocating its
justification. The discipline rests on TFR observations running below medium-variant projections;
this paper adds an independent reason to distrust apparent dividend headroom — the chronological
measure of that headroom is itself inflated where functional capacity is low. Flagged
`baseline-revision` on that basis, provisionally, pending Anne.

## Caveats

- **Reduced-form growth model, not structural.** No pension system, no tax, no fiscal closure. It
  speaks to growth, not to contributory balances — the same limit as Acemoglu et al., though with
  a better-aligned outcome variable.
- **Projections extrapolate exogenous growth trends.** The authors' defence is sound: the trends
  enter every scenario identically, so scenario *differences* — which is what they report — are
  insensitive to the extrapolation.
- **Capital formation abstracted from in the baseline.** Appendix B.9 endogenises it and finds
  the response "somewhat augments," so the headline estimates are if anything conservative.
- **The prospective bound is a potential, not a forecast.** It measures what could be offset if
  working life extended with functional capacity, not what will be.
- **Robustness is extensive and clean**: alternative specifications, calibrated parameters,
  shorter horizons (2020–2030 and 2020–2040), alternative development paths, endogenous capital,
  and workforce shares in place of working-age shares. Under fast development, functional-capacity
  improvements fully counteract the drag; under slow development, not at all. Main results sit in
  the middle.

## Project Routing Notes

**BDH — primary, unusually.** This is the most BDH-relevant paper of the seven. Functional
capacity, healthy ageing, disability, morbidity and remaining life expectancy are the operative
variables, and the finding that promoting healthy ageing has sizable economic gains over and
above human welfare is directly a health-financing argument. The prospective-measure machinery
(Sanderson-Scherbov POAT) is a tool BDH could adopt. Data sources include ELSA, HRS and SHARE.
Route to Beth.

**Aurora.** Secondary. The functional-capacity offset is a structural force operating on the
same timescale as the technological drivers, and its realisation is institutionally contingent
— which is Aurora's kind of question.

**CROSS-TAR-001.** "Prospective old age threshold" (POAT) and the retrospective/prospective
distinction are strong anchor candidates if the measurement question is taken up, precisely
because adopting them departs from NTA convention and the departure must be legible across
projects.
