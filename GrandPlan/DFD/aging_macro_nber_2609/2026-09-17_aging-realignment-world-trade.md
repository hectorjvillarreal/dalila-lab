## Summary

Joseph Kopecky (Trinity College Dublin) embeds an age-skill mechanism into the multi-sector
Eaton–Kortum framework of Caliendo and Parro (2015). Workers supply three age-dependent task
inputs — age-appreciating cognitive (verbal, comprehension; rising to the mid-50s),
age-depreciating cognitive (memory, processing speed), and physical — with sectoral cost shares
constructed from O*NET ability ratings and BLS occupational employment, following Cai and
Stoyanov (2016). A country's age structure therefore determines its effective factor
endowments, so ageing rotates comparative advantage across sectors ("life-cycle comparative
advantage"). Thirty economies, twenty-one sectors, WIOD 2016 release (2014 base), UN WPP 2024
demographics, counterfactuals via exact hat algebra.

Headline: by 2050 demographics alone lower China's exports to the US by 9% and raise India's by
33%. Volume moves are almost entirely a **scale** effect (workforce size); the composition
channel carries roughly 2% of corridor moves and never shifts one by more than a percentage
point. Composition does reorganise sectors within corridors — losses concentrate in
physical/depreciating-intensive sectors (textiles −16.8%, basic metals −17.7%) and spare
appreciating-intensive ones (computers and electronics −0.4%), fitting appreciating-skill
intensity at R² = 0.90.

A minimal saving block turns ageing advanced and East Asian economies into net creditors and the
young into debtors, correlating +0.60 with observed 2014 NFA positions, and leaves the corridor
results essentially unchanged (correlation 0.994). Composition welfare is small and largely
self-cancelling (a traded-goods price cost offset by a non-traded services price benefit),
within roughly ±0.2% everywhere.

**This is the first of the five NBER-conference papers in which Mexico is a named headline
economy rather than a case requiring extrapolation.**

> **Metric caution (Cath, 2026-09-17).** The welfare figures above are the paper's own
> terminology: percentage changes in real income and in composition-adjusted consumption
> baskets. They are not consumption-equivalent variation. Under DFD welfare-metric discipline
> they may be cited as output or real-income changes and **must not be carried into DFD output
> as welfare results**.

- Historical welfare (Table 7): Mexico under 1970 demographics = 0.223 of its 2014 level, the
  **lowest figure of any country in the sample** — Mexico's demographic dividend was the largest
  in the set.
- Figure 4: the average Mexican goods worker faces roughly +31% export change by 2050, with
  nearly all sectors rising rather than straddling zero.
- Composition welfare: goods-only −0.82%, goods-CPI cost −0.30%, services benefit +0.43%, net
  **+0.13%**.
- Total demographic welfare (workforce-size channel, capital and technology fixed): Mexico
  **+36%** by 2050, against Japan −35%, Korea −40%, China −29%.

**The paper places Mexico firmly on the winning side of the demographic trade realignment
through 2050.** This runs against DFD's framing and should be engaged rather than discounted.

## DFD Calibration Implications

**Fast-transition TFR scenario for Mexico** — the model uses UN WPP 2024 medium variant, which
DFD treats as the optimistic case rather than the central one. But the critique bites only
weakly at this horizon, and it is important to be precise about why: Mexico's 2050 working-age
population (ages 20–64) comprises cohorts born 1986–2030, of which only the 2026–2030 births
are unborn today — one of nine five-year groups. **Mexico's demographic trade tailwind through
2050 is largely locked in by cohorts already born, and the paper is probably right about it.**

What the paper does not show, because it stops at 2050, is that this is the last of it. What
Kopecky documents for China in his window — dividend cresting, then reversing — is Mexico's
2050–2080 window. The reversal is deferred beyond the projection horizon, not avoided.

**Dependency ratio / IM-6** — no direct implication. Trade volumes are carried by workforce
size, which IM-6 already tracks. The paper's contribution is compositional and operates on a
margin IM-6 does not model.

**The volume result is close to mechanical.** With composition carrying ~2% of corridor moves
(for Mexico, +0.2pp of +27.1), the headline reduces to *countries with more workers export
more*. Not a criticism of the paper — the composition channel is its genuine contribution and
does reorganise sectors — but the Mexico headline is a statement about workforce size, not about
competitiveness.

**Mexico's gain is partly a US policy variable.** Under the UN zero-migration variant (Table 5),
MEX→USA falls from +27.1% to +20.7%. The mechanism operates on the US side: without immigration
the US working-age population stops growing and imports less from every source. A ~6pp swing in
Mexico's tailwind depends on US immigration policy, not Mexican demography. Connects directly to
Bernardino, Franco & Teles Morais (filed same day) on the fiscal politics of restricting
migration.

## The Finding Most Relevant to DFD

**Mexico's demographic borrowing window and its demographic fiscal burden are adversely
sequenced.**

Table 3: Mexico's net foreign asset position moves from −0.33 to −0.43 of GDP by 2050 — the
largest debtor shift in the sample, from an already-largest debtor position. Simultaneously,
ageing in advanced and East Asian economies produces a savings glut; the model puts the world
interest rate 35% below base by 2050.

So Mexico borrows cheaply and increasingly **precisely while its dividend is open**, and
financing terms deteriorate exactly as its own dependency ratio begins rising. Cheap external
finance is available during the decades of lowest fiscal pressure and withdraws as pressure
arrives.

This is a debt-dynamics observation that none of the prior four papers reaches. It sits in the
fiscal dominance line and is as much Nina's lane as Anne's or Cath's. It also suggests a
sharper framing of the fiscal-space question than the conventional static one.

**Honesty flag on this result.** Kopecky is unusually candid about the saving block: US-only
wealth and labour-income age profiles, placeholder interest elasticities (ε = 0.3, σ = 1.0) and
annuitisation rate (0.04), and — his own statement — "the cross-section therefore validates the
model's positions, not the demographic saving mechanism specifically," because most of the
cross-country fit comes from the capital–output ratio κc rather than the demographic term ωc.
The direction is disciplined; the magnitude is not. The 35% interest-rate fall is quoted by the
author "to fix ideas rather than as an estimate."

## Pension-Reform-Adjacent Result

Section 6.3: extending effective retirement to include the 65–69 group recovers large
workforce-size welfare (Japan 11.3pp, Germany 8.7, US 8.6, Korea 7.0) but barely moves the
composition channel, never exceeding 0.4% in absolute value. Kopecky's conclusion: retirement
extension is an effective answer to the workforce-size problem but not to the competitiveness
problem; the latter would require rebalancing the effective skill mix through younger-worker
immigration or through retraining that remaps age to task inputs.

Useful when retirement-age extension arises in the Mexican pension discussion, with the caveat
that this is an output-side result and silent on the contributory balance — which is where
Beth's question lives.

## Further Limitations

- **Frictionless.** Section 4.4 counts the workers: 97% of Japan's 9.7m goods-sector employees
  and 91% of Korea's 3.7m are in industries whose exports fall more than 10%; 35m in China,
  ~24m across the six fastest-ageing economies. The model prices the consumption basket, not
  the reallocation, and Kopecky says so explicitly.
- **Capital held fixed as a stock and as a factor.** Both the GE feedback from the falling world
  rate and a capital–skill reallocation channel are outside the trade equilibrium.
- **Demand is age-independent.** Expenditure shares fixed, so the supply-side services channel
  is isolated and the demand-side channel (ageing populations consuming more non-traded
  services, which runs the opposite way — Goodhart and Pradhan 2020, Juselius and Takáts 2018)
  is absent.
- **The developing world is a rest-of-world composite** in the 30-economy WIOD aggregation;
  Kopecky flags Sub-Saharan Africa as the most important omission.
- **Composition-welfare sign is the fragile object**, flipping under skill-dispersion
  compression and under dropping employment weights. Corridor signs and rankings are robust
  across all twelve perturbations in Table 5 (correlations ≥ 0.97).

## Project Routing Notes

**Aurora.** The "China-plus-one" connection is explicit and useful: Kopecky notes the model has
no geopolitical machinery, yet pushes the same corridors in the same direction through each
country's own ageing — and that estimates of policy-driven realignment which omit the
demographic trend will fold part of it into the treatment effect. Demography as a confound in
geopolitical realignment analysis is directly Gina's and Elle's territory. Note also that the US
market share released by China's ageing does **not** reroute to India or Mexico: over half is
absorbed by domestic US production.
