## Purpose

Consolidates the cross-paper argument arising from six papers obtained in connection with the
NBER virtual conference on the macroeconomic effects of population aging (Auclert et al.,
September 2026). Supersedes the preliminary synthesis table in
`2026-09-17_baby-busts-growth-booms.md`. Individual paper notes carry the detail; this note
carries the argument.

**Nothing here is settled. The framing proposed in Section 3 requires Anne's endorsement, and
the IM-6 implications require Cath's.**

## 1. The Six Papers

| Note | Paper | Method | Margin identified |
|---|---|---|---|
| `costs-of-building-walls` | Bernardino, Franco & Teles Morais | Cohort-component projection + fiscal age profiles, EA | Migration |
| `demographic-cliff-higher-education` | Krueger, Ludwig & Popova | GE life-cycle college market, US | Education / skill |
| `baby-busts-growth-booms` | Acemoglu, Autor, Beirne & Scott | Reduced form, cross-country + US commuting zones | Technology (directed technical change) |
| `endogenous-technical-change` | Pettersson | OLG + semi-endogenous R&D growth, US | Technology (savings → R&D) |
| `aging-realignment-world-trade` | Kopecky | Multi-sector Eaton–Kortum with age-skill endowments, 30 economies | Trade reorientation / comparative advantage |
| `global-transition-demographics-ai` | Benzell, Kotlikoff & Ye | Multi-region life-cycle CGE, 17 regions, endogenous automation | Automation adoption + capital mobility |

Two of the six name Mexico as a distinct economy: Kopecky and Benzell-Kotlikoff-Ye. **They
disagree about it** (Section 4a).

Benzell-Kotlikoff-Ye is assessed as the most DFD-relevant of the six: the only one carrying a
Mexico region, an explicit payroll-tax and pension result, and endogenous automation
simultaneously, and the only one in IM-6's own Auerbach-Kotlikoff lineage.

**Related material, different provenance:** `dividend-to-drag-kotschy-bloom` — Kotschy & Bloom
(31 March 2026), 145 countries, 1950–2015 estimation and 2020–2050 projection. Not from the
conference program as far as can be established. It matters here because it is the only source
in the set that poses the dividend-to-drag question directly and globally, and it partly closes
the gap identified in Section 2a. See Section 2b.

**Also filed, peripheral:** `spatial-consequences-depopulation` — de Silva & Paron (Stanford GSB,
21 August 2026), on depopulation and spatial concentration. No fiscal system, no age structure,
Japanese calibration; it changes nothing here. Retained for a planner result favouring transfers
*from* sparse *to* dense regions, against the grain of every existing depopulation policy, and an
appendix showing that housing-driven fertility recovery fails to stabilise population under CES
housing production. Its subnational-fiscal workstream proposal is superseded by the next entry.

**Also filed, substantial:** `ghost-town-geography-depopulation-aging` — Giannone, Miyauchi,
Paixão, Pang & Suzuki (September 2026). Japanese municipality data plus a dynamic life-cycle
spatial GE model in which a national government finances pensions *and* local public services
from labour income tax. **The only item in the set carrying local public finance as a modelled
object.** Identified result: a 1% rise in working-age population lowers municipal spending per
capita by 0.53% while personal income tax per capita does not respond — so population decline
widens local fiscal deficits and raises reliance on national transfers. Amenities (including
health/medical) decline where working-age population is lost. Spatial divergence *intensifies*
under aggregate decline rather than equalising.

This opens a **subnational fiscal geography** line for DFD that none of the other eight reach.
Mexico's structure is closer to Japan's than to the US cases dominating this literature, and the
revenue result should bind harder here given how little *predial* municipios collect.
Population-weighted Ramo 28 and Ramo 33 formulas run against a non-proportional cost function.
One Mexican feature is absent from every model in the set: rural municipios lose working-age
population to internal *and* international destinations simultaneously, with remittances
returning as income that is fiscally invisible and generates no contributory accrual.

**Note on the growth-versus-fiscal wedge.** It appears in six or seven of the eight papers, most
starkly where the headline is most favourable: Acemoglu (GDP per working-age adult up, fiscal
silent), Pettersson (output per capita +27.5%, contribution rate quintuples), Kopecky (Mexican
welfare +36%, NFA position deteriorating), de Silva & Paron (GDP per capita +27%, subnational tax
rates rising fastest where population empties). **This is a structural feature of how the
literature is constructed, not a paper-by-paper caveat, and it is the principal reason DFD's
premise survives a literature that mostly reports good news.**

## 2. The Common Structure

All four are about **adjustment margins that offset the mechanical arithmetic of ageing**. Each
identifies a different margin, and each finds it substantial. Read together they constitute a
serious collective challenge to the naive projection in which a rising dependency ratio
translates directly into fiscal pressure.

| Paper | Margin | Timing profile | Verdict on the margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bernardino et al. | Migration | Immediate; fertility recovery far too late | Helps, convexly; cannot solve |
| Krueger et al. | Education / skill | Early, expectation-driven | Helps; institutionally contingent |
| Acemoglu et al. | Technology (induced automation) | Multi-decade, historical | Helps per-worker; fiscally untested |
| Pettersson | Technology (savings → R&D) | Multi-decade | Helps; two-thirds is a population-scale effect |
| Kopecky | Trade / comparative advantage | Contemporaneous with workforce size | Reorganises sectors; volumes are near-mechanical |

**Timing is the recurring analytical object.** Fertility fails on timing (dependents now,
contributors in 25 years). Education succeeds on timing (expectation-driven reallocation, not
cohort arrival). Migration succeeds immediately but with diminishing returns. The R&D and
automation channels operate over decades. The fiscal value of any margin is governed by *when*
it delivers relative to when the burden arrives — a first-order quantitative question, not a
refinement.

### 2a. The projection windows close before the LAC burden arrives

Three of the five papers reach favourable conclusions partly through where their window ends:

- **Acemoglu et al.** identify off falling birth rates from 1950, in economies whose working-age
  share was *rising* for most of the period. The estimation window is predominantly the
  demographic dividend phase.
- **Krueger et al.** impose terminal stationarity (n = 1% → n = 0%), so the long run is a common
  age structure and the cliff is "close to a pure scale effect." Pettersson does the same, and
  explicitly sets aside declining-population dynamics (his footnote 4, citing Jones 2022a).
- **Kopecky** stops at 2050. Mexico's working-age population in 2050 comprises cohorts born
  1986–2030, of which only 2026–2030 births are unborn — so his favourable Mexico result is
  largely locked in and probably correct. What he documents for China in his window (dividend
  cresting, then reversing) is Mexico's 2050–2080 window.

**This is a methodological finding, not a collection of individual caveats: the frontier
literature's windows systematically close before the Latin American burden arrives.** Papers
that place Mexico on the winning side do so because their horizons end while Mexico is still in
the dividend phase. DFD's object is what comes after, and that period is outside the support of
every result in this set.

### 2b. The measurement of age structure flatters both phases

Kotschy & Bloom (related material, filed separately) partly close the Section 2a gap by posing
the dividend-to-drag question directly across 145 countries. Their projection: **drag is the
norm** — age-structure shifts slow growth in 70 sample countries, virtually all those whose
working-age shares contract. OECD growth slows 0.8pp on chronological measures.

Their methodological contribution matters more to DFD than the headline. Distinguishing
**retrospective** (chronological) from **prospective** age structure — where the old-age
threshold is set by remaining life expectancy and tracks functional capacity — the correction
flips sign by phase:

- **Drag phase:** prospective gives a *smaller* drag (OECD 0.8pp → 0.4pp; Japan −0.89 → −0.13).
  Up to half compensable, but only where remaining life expectancy at older ages is high.
- **Dividend phase:** prospective gives a *smaller and shorter* dividend, because labour
  potential is lower where functional capacity is low.

**Chronological measures flatter both ends.** For Mexico this bites twice over: the remaining
dividend is smaller than it looks, and when the drag arrives less of it is compensable — both
because functional capacity at older ages sits below the OECD leaders'. Mexico is disadvantaged
on both sides of a single parameter.

This is a stronger statement of DFD's premise than Section 3 reaches by the margins argument,
and it belongs to a paper rather than to us. It also raises a live question for the demographic
module: NTA age profiles are chronological by construction, so adopting prospective measures
would be a documented departure from NTA convention under DFD's own methodology principles.
**Anne's authority; posed as a question, not a recommendation.**

## 3. Proposed DFD Position

**The mechanical arithmetic of ageing is an upper bound on fiscal pressure. The research
question is which adjustment margins are actually available in a middle-income,
high-informality, technology-adopting economy undergoing a compressed transition.**

Mexico plausibly has weak access to all four:

- **Migration** — net emigration rather than immigration. Bernardino et al.'s Proposition 1
  sign-flips: the marginal fiscal cost of working-age emigration is *increasing* in the outflow.
- **Education** — the price channel through which Krueger et al.'s enrollment response operates
  is largely absent in the public flagship segment, which rations by entrance exam.
- **Induced automation** — concentrated in a formal sector covering less than half the workforce
  (see the informality hypothesis in Section 5).
- **Savings → R&D** — requires a growing population (scale effect), a technology frontier
  position, and a working life-cycle savings motive. Mexico has none of the three.

This framing is stronger and more defensible than asserting that ageing is fiscally costly. It
converts DFD's premise from an assumption into a research question with a testable answer, and
it locates DFD's contribution precisely where the frontier literature has a gap.

## 4. Growth Optimism and Fiscal Pessimism Are Not in Conflict

The apparent tension between the growth-optimistic papers and DFD's fiscal-pressure premise
dissolves once the outcome variables are separated.

Acemoglu et al.'s headline is **GDP per working-age adult**. The fiscal problem of ageing is
precisely that the ratio of working-age adults to everyone else falls. GDP per worker rising and
the tax burden per worker rising are fully compatible — each worker supports more dependents.
Their aggregate-GDP estimate, the one that would bear on the fiscal question, is
β = −0.006 (SE 0.130): a failure to reject zero, not evidence of zero.

**Pettersson settles this inside a single model.** Carrying both channels: output per capita
rises 27.5% *and* the social security contribution rate rises from ~4% to ~22.5% by 2100. This
is the cleanest available demonstration, and it comes from a paper whose headline is
growth-optimistic.

**Implication for DFD's rhetoric:** do not contest the growth findings. Concede them and point
out that they are answers to a different question. That is both correct and stronger.

**The fiscal magnitude is now on record.** Benzell, Kotlikoff & Ye supply what the other five
omit or reach only partially: under GNI-indexed benefits, the world payroll tax rate roughly
doubles (13.1% → 25.4% of covered payroll by 2100), world pension outlays rise from 5.8% to
11.9% of GDP, and the effective average tax burden on total income goes from 31.4% to 40.8%
worldwide (47.0% US, 56.7% Western Europe). China's payroll rate reaches 38.1% under the 2024
vintage. This is the reference point for what ageing does to contributory systems.

## 4a. The Two Papers That Name Mexico Disagree About It

| | Kopecky | Benzell, Kotlikoff & Ye |
|---|---|---|
| Mexico by 2050/2100 | Exports to US +27%; average goods worker +31%; welfare +36% | World GNI share 2.1% → 1.9% → 1.3% |
| What moves | Trade volumes, technology and capital fixed | Output share, endogenous automation and mobile capital |
| Why | Mexican workforce still growing relative to the world | Mexico never adopts frontier technology; loses the competition for mobile capital |

Not strictly contradictory — different outcome variables and different things held fixed — but
the mechanism separating them is exactly DFD's concern. **Which is right about Mexico turns on
whether capital mobility and frontier adoption dominate workforce size.** Answerable, central,
and proposed as a priority item for Anne and Cath.

Benzell-Kotlikoff-Ye also bears on Section 5 item 2 below. Their Mexico never adopts frontier
automation (BAU, both vintages; 2097 only under 2024 demographics plus accelerated AI) because
Mexican labour is too cheap — in a model with **no informality at all**. Two independent
mechanisms, one conclusion. This strengthens the finding that the automation offset is
unavailable to Mexico and narrows the originality of the informality argument: informality is an
additional reason, not the reason.

## 5. Original Lines Arising (all requiring Anne's assessment)

1. **Emigration convexity.** Bernardino et al.'s Proposition 1, mirrored, implies an increasing
   marginal fiscal cost of working-age emigration for LAC. Requires no new machinery in the
   demographic module. Apparently unwritten for the region.

2. **Informality reverses the automation sign.** If labour scarcity induces automation, it does
   so in formal firms; informal firms do not automate. Labour scarcity in Mexico could raise
   formal-sector productivity and measured GDP per worker while displacing labour into
   informality, *shrinking* the contributory base. One would observe the Acemoglu growth result
   and a deteriorating fiscal position as two faces of the same adjustment.

3. **Education raises skill and formality jointly.** Education is among the strongest predictors
   of formality, so an enrollment response would widen the tax base twice over. The fiscal
   cushion could be larger in Mexico than the US even if the enrollment response is weaker.

4. **Frontier-ageing spillover.** If technology is nonrival and diffuses internationally,
   advanced-economy ageing raising frontier R&D is a positive externality for adopting
   economies — Mexico receives innovation without financing it. Unmodelled in all five papers.
   Aurora more than DFD.

5. **Adverse sequencing of the borrowing window and the fiscal burden.** Kopecky's Table 3 puts
   Mexico's net foreign asset position at −0.33 of GDP in 2014 and −0.43 by 2050, the largest
   debtor shift in his sample, while advanced and East Asian ageing produces a savings glut
   (world rate 35% below base by 2050 in his calibration). Mexico therefore borrows cheaply and
   increasingly **precisely while its dividend is open**, with terms deteriorating exactly as
   its own dependency ratio starts rising. Cheap external finance is available during the
   decades of lowest fiscal pressure and withdraws as pressure arrives. A debt-dynamics
   observation none of the other four reaches; sits in the fiscal dominance line and is as much
   Nina's lane as Anne's or Cath's. Caveat: Kopecky's saving block uses US-only profiles and
   placeholder elasticities, and he states that his cross-section validates the block's
   positions rather than the demographic mechanism specifically. Direction disciplined,
   magnitude not.

6. **Subnational fiscal geography under decline — the two-door variant.** Giannone et al.
   establish for Japan that shrinking municipalities face rising per-capita service costs and
   flat per-capita own revenue, widening local deficits and raising reliance on national
   transfers. Mexico's arrangement is structurally similar and the revenue side binds harder
   (thin *predial* collection), with Ramo 28 and Ramo 33 formulas population-weighted against a
   non-proportional cost function. The Mexican extension absent from every model in the set:
   rural municipios lose working-age population **both** internally and internationally, and the
   remittances flowing back support consumption while generating no local fiscal capacity and no
   contributory pension accrual. Connects to item 1 (emigration convexity) and to the informality
   thread. Anne and Cath.

Items 2 and 3 both sit at the Anne–Cath–Diego intersection with the conditional logit
formality-choice work (ENIGH 2022).

## 6. Methodological Findings (proposed for methodology-principles)

**a. Timing of adjustment margins.** As Section 2. The fiscal value of a margin is governed by
when it delivers relative to when the burden arrives.

**b. The pension closure rule is a determinant of results, not a convention.** Pettersson closes
through the contribution rate (fixed replacement rate), explicitly the growth-pessimistic
choice. Krueger, Ludwig & Popova close through the replacement rate, and that falling rate
generates part of their education response. Opposite closures at the same conference, each
working in the direction of its paper's mechanism.

Benzell, Kotlikoff & Ye now quantify it, in IM-6's own lineage (their Table A16): the world 2100
payroll rate is **25.4% under GNI-indexed benefits against 19.7% under official legislated
projections** — 5.7 percentage points from the indexation rule alone — and preserving generosity
costs ~2% of world GDP in 2050 and ~2.5% by 2100 through crowding out labour supply and young
workers' saving.

Any paper not reporting sensitivity to the closure should be read accordingly — and IM-6's own
closure is genuinely ambiguous given a defined-contribution system plus an expanding
non-contributory pillar.

**b2. A caution on the fast-transition discipline itself.** Benzell, Kotlikoff & Ye report that
regions revised *upward* between WPP 2017 and WPP 2024 see 2100 burdens fall, and name **Mexico**
among them (~1pp lower payroll rate). This implies the UN revised Mexico up, which runs against
the discipline's premise that UN and CELADE medium variants are optimistic for Mexico.

Concrete, answerable from data held: **what TFR path does WPP 2024 medium assume for Mexico, how
does it compare to observed values (~1.55) and to CELADE, and in which direction did the
2017→2024 revision move Mexico?** If the revision was upward while observed TFR sits near 1.55,
the discipline needs a Mexico-specific defence. Not resolved in either direction here. **Anne's
authority; proposed as the single highest-priority verification item in this set.**

**c. Endogenous versus exogenous TFP is worth ~40 points of cumulative output.** Pettersson's
Section 6.4: the same transition yields +27.5% with endogenous technology and −12.2% with it
shut down (the latter matching Krueger and Ludwig 2007). Whatever IM-6 assumes, the assumption
should be stated and its cost acknowledged.

**d. Model-internal fertility parameters are not observed TFRs.** Krueger et al. calibrate
ζ̄ = 1.14 and 1.01 in a unisex normalised model. These are growth-consistent indices, not
children per woman, and will be misread beside Bernardino's 1.6 or Mexico's 1.55.

**e. Terminal stationarity is often imposed, not derived.** Krueger et al. impose convergence to
n = 0; Pettersson imposes identical population growth in both terminal steady states and
explicitly sets aside declining-population dynamics (his footnote 4, citing Jones 2022a). Under
fast-transition discipline, sustained decline *is* the DFD scenario. Bernardino et al. are the
only ones who handle sub-replacement fertility honestly (extinction paths,
stationary-through-immigration).

## 7. Standing Reference in Dispute

Acemoglu et al. cite Fernández-Villaverde et al. (2025) as representative of the view they argue
against. Fernández-Villaverde is the standing scenario-discipline reference in this monitoring
protocol. The disagreement concerns the *macroeconomic consequences* of ageing, not the
demographic facts, so it does not touch the fast-transition TFR discipline. Neither should be
cited as settled without noting the other.
