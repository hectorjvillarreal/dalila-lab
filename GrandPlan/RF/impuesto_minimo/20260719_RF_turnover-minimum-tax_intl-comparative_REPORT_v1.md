# Turnover-based corporate minimum taxes: international comparative

**Verification legend.** ✓✓ = claim survived a 3-voter adversarial panel (verified
verbatim against the primary source; votes shown as e.g. 3-0). ✓ = extracted from
the named source with supporting quote, but the adversarial vote did not complete
(session interruptions); treat as sourced-but-unaudited. ✗ = adversarial panel
**refuted** the claim; the correction is reported instead. Facts with no symbol are
arithmetic on verified inputs.

**The one-metric spine.** Under liability = max(t·turnover, τ·profit), the implied
presumptive **margin floor** is t/τ. Mexico's illustrative 2%/30% → **6.67%**.
The IMF's cross-country survey states this equivalence explicitly: a 1% turnover
minimum equals a 30% profits tax for a firm with a ~3% margin, and any firm whose
margin is below t/τ pays more than under the CIT (✓✓ 3-0, IMF WP/21/161).

## 2. Per-country notes (bucket A and instructive cases)

### 2.1 Pakistan — the deepest empirical record (prioritized per brief §4)

*Sources: Best, Brockmeyer, Kleven, Spinnewijn & Waseem, JPE 123(6) 2015 (primary,
full text verified); Javid Law Associates on ITO 2001 §113 (practitioner).*

- **Mechanics** ✓✓ (3-0): in force since 1991; every firm computes both a 35%
  profit-tax and a 0.5% turnover-tax liability and pays the larger — the exact
  bucket-A max() structure. Margin-floor kink at 0.5/35 = **1.43%** (2.5% for
  reduced-rate 20% CIT firms). The kink **moved with policy**: bunching at 1.43%
  vanished in 2008 when the minimum was temporarily removed and reappeared at 2.86%
  when the turnover rate doubled to 1% in 2010 (✓ — non-parametric proof firms
  manage reported margins to the t/τ ratio).
- **Creditability** ✓✓ (3-0): excess minimum tax carries forward against future
  profit tax up to 5 years (3 in 2008–09) — but **only 1.3% of firms ever claim
  the carry-forward**, so the instrument is creditable in form, definitive in
  practice.
- **Fiscal dominance** ✓ (1-0, quote verified): 50–60% of corporations are liable
  under the turnover regime, accounting for 60–75% of all corporate tax revenue —
  a "minimum" that became the main corporate tax.
- **Performance evidence** ✓✓ (3-0, two independent claim-formulations each 3-0):
  bunching design on the universe of corporate returns 2006–2010 estimates turnover
  taxation **reduces evasion by up to 60–70% of corporate income** relative to
  profit taxation; a calibrated switch from profit to (much lower rate) turnover
  taxation would **raise corporate tax revenue by 74% without reducing aggregate
  after-tax profits**. The authors argue evasion heterogeneity itself rationalizes
  the max() structure (✓✓ 2-0).
- **Current statute** ✓ (unaudited): ITO 2001 §113 now levies 1.25% of gross
  turnover (0.5% for specified industries), no size threshold; losses carry forward
  six years but loss-year firms still pay the minimum.
- **Context caveat**: footnote 2 of the JPE paper lists ~30 countries with similar
  schemes incl. Guatemala, Honduras, Panama, Mexico, Colombia, Ecuador,
  El Salvador, and francophone Africa (Cameroon, Chad, Gabon, Côte d'Ivoire,
  Senegal, Tunisia) ✓.

### 2.2 Honduras — the closest structural analog

*Sources: Scot, "Trading losses? Minimum taxation in Honduras" (JMP / working
paper, universe of Honduran corporate filings); World Bank blogs; IMF WP/21/161
(✓✓ for the revenue result).*

- **Mechanics** ✓ (extraction; votes interrupted): FY2014, part of the anti-evasion
  tax package — corporations declaring gross revenue above L10 million (~USD 400k)
  pay max(25% CIT, 1.5% of gross revenue). Margin floor exactly **6%** — the direct
  analog of Mexico's 6.67%. IMF WP/21/161 records the fuller structure as
  max(1.5% turnover if >L10M, 1% net assets, CIT) ✓✓ (3-0).
- **Revenue** ✓✓ (3-0, IMF WP/21/161): revenue from firms made newly liable rose
  **almost 1% of GDP in 2016**. Scot: total corporate tax revenue +up to 30%,
  aggregate corporate profits −10% ✓.
- **What it revealed about evasion** ✓: under pure profit taxation (pre-2014),
  large firms overreported costs by 13–17% of profits, concentrated in
  hard-to-verify items (inventories, not payroll or finance); after 2014, affected
  firms began reporting much higher margins (+0.9–1.1 pp).
- **New distortion margins** ✓: (i) bunching just below the L10M threshold, present
  only in years the minimum was in force; marginal bunchers cut reported revenue
  15–30%; (ii) reported-revenue elasticity 0.35–1.0 — gross revenue is itself
  manipulable — **strongly attenuated where third-party information on revenue
  exists** (excess mass 65% larger for low-observability firms). Direct read-across
  to CFDI: the erosion resistance of a turnover minimum is conditional on the
  revenue-traceability infrastructure.
- **Escape valves** ✓: reduced 0.75% rate for cement, state enterprises,
  pharmaceuticals, bakeries; exemption for first two years of operation and
  petroleum sectors; loss-declaring firms exempt with a net-asset tax as backstop.
- **Litigation & trajectory** ✓: a 2011 1% minimum was struck down by the Supreme
  Court and never took effect; the 2014 version was challenged and **upheld in
  2015**; then politically neutered from FY2018 by raising the exemption threshold
  from L10M to L300M and later L600M — erosion by threshold, not by rate.

### 2.3 Panama — CAIR, the escape-valve model

*Sources: PwC Panama corporate tax summary (current); CEPAL Serie Macroeconomía
del desarrollo (Cetrángolo & Gómez Sabaini 2007, primary PDF verified); Gaceta
Oficial text of Ley 6 de 2005 (verified by a voter).*

- **Mechanics** ✓✓ (3-0 on the CEPAL-era formulation): CAIR requires computing CIT
  twice — on net taxable income and on a presumptive **4.67% of gross taxable
  income** — and paying the greater. At adoption (2005–07): threshold B/.150,000
  gross income, 30% CIT → effective ≈1.4% of gross. Currently ✓: 25% CIT, threshold
  USD 1.5M taxable income → effective ≈1.17% of gross. The **statutory presumptive
  margin floor is 4.67%** by construction. Base excludes exempt, non-taxable and
  foreign-source income ✓.
- **Escape valve — the headline feature** ✓ (two formulations; CEPAL variant inside
  the ✓✓ record): a taxpayer may petition the DGI for non-application of CAIR if
  the alternative calculation would produce/deepen a loss **or** push its effective
  rate above the statutory 25%; the petition is **deemed granted if not decided
  within six months**. This is §5.8 of the brief made real: margin heterogeneity
  handled case-by-case, with the administrative-delay risk shifted to the state.
- **Verdict**: in force; the design most directly responsive to the
  thin-margin-honest-firm problem.

### 2.4 Guatemala — IEMA (corrected record; the ✗ case)

The adversarial panel **refuted** the first-draft claim (0-3) and produced the
corrected record: IEMA (impuesto a las empresas mercantiles y agrícolas,
Decreto 99-98 of 1998) was introduced at **2.5% on net assets or 1.25% on gross
income** (taxpayer-elected), **raised in 2000 to 3.5%/2.25%**; it collected
~1.4–1.6% of GDP in 2002 (about half of income-tax revenue); the Corte de
Constitucionalidad declared it unconstitutional (ruling of 15 Dec 2003, effects
early 2004); its actual successor was **IETAAP** (June 2004), not the ordinary ISR.
A CEPAL survey (✓✓ 3-0) records the regional pattern this sits in: asset minimums
clustering at 1.0% with a mid-2000s shift toward gross-income bases, Belize running
sector-differentiated turnover rates of 0.75%–25%, and warns these regimes erode.
Costa Rica's 1% creditable asset tax (Ley de Justicia Tributaria 1995) was struck
down in 2000 with forced refunds ✓✓ (3-0, same record as the Guatemala
strike-down claim).

### 2.5 Peru — the constitutional pole (and a bucket correction to the brief)

*Sources: Tribunal Constitucional primary rulings — STC 0646-1996-AA/TC,
STC 2727-2002-AA/TC (19 Dec 2003), STC 0033-2004-AI/TC (28 Sep 2004). Claims ✓
(extraction from primary texts; panel votes interrupted).*

- **Correction to §4 of the brief**: Peru's famous struck-down minimum, the
  **Impuesto Mínimo a la Renta**, was levied at **2% of net assets** — bucket B,
  not a turnover minimum. The doctrine, however, transfers.
- **The doctrine**: the IMR fell not because taxing patrimony is per se forbidden,
  but because of the **means–end incongruence** of embedding a source-taxing
  minimum inside a law whose declared object is income (capacidad contributiva);
  for taxes on income, only actually generated income may index capacity, and
  **loss years are constitutionally decisive** (confiscation where the levy hits
  the productive source). The AAIR was struck a fortiori in 2004 (payable
  regardless of income). Meanwhile the Tribunal **upheld** the standalone IEAN
  (Ley 26777, 0.5% net assets, temporary, creditable-without-refund) — expressly
  citing its standalone character, base deductions, and creditability.
- **Transfer to the Mexican instrument**: the vulnerability is not the turnover
  base; it is (i) framing the minimum *inside* the ISR as an income tax while its
  base is not income, and (ii) binding loss-making firms with no relief. A
  standalone levy, creditable, with a loss/low-margin valve, is the configuration
  that survived in Lima.

### 2.6 Mexico's own precedent — SCJN on gross-income bases

*Source: SCJN Comunicado 034/2010 (9 Feb 2010), Pleno. Claims ✓ (extraction from
the SCJN's own text; votes interrupted).*

The Pleno denied the IETU amparos, holding the tax consistent with
proporcionalidad and equidad; it characterized the taxed manifestation of wealth
as **gross income (ingresos brutos)** — i.e., Mexican doctrine accepts gross
receipts as a legitimate index of contributive capacity — and held that structural
denial of deductions (payroll, PTU, interest, related-party royalties) is a design
element, not arbitrariness. This is the opposite pole from Peru and the domestic
anchor for a turnover-base instrument. (Consistent with the SCJN's IETU
gross-income holding already noted in the brief's colophon.)

### 2.7 France — IFA lifecycle (bucket-A adjacent)

*Source: fr.wikipedia (statutory history; unaudited ✓).* Created 27 Dec 1973;
amount set as **fixed sums per turnover bracket** (final schedule: €0 below €15M;
€20,500 for €15–75M; €32,750 for €75–500M; €110,000 above €500M — trivially small
effective rates). Originally **creditable** against IS; from 2006 **deductible
only** (definitive); phased out from 2009 (first exemption tranche cost ~€336M)
and abolished 1 Jan 2014. No low-margin escape valve — only a 3-year new-company
exemption. Lifecycle: creditable minimum → definitive surcharge → repeal.

### 2.8 United States — the gross-receipts laboratory

*Sources: Tax Foundation (two reports), WA Dept. of Revenue 2002 committee study,
Texas Margin Tax report. ✓ unless noted.*

- **As minimums**: New Jersey's 2002 **Alternative Minimum Assessment** had the
  exact max(GRT, CIT) structure (0.125%–0.4% on receipts over $2M) — repealed by
  2006. Kentucky's GRT-based AMT repealed 2006 after the state's own forecaster
  found it reduced investment and harmed small business; Indiana 2002; Michigan
  MBT 2011.
- **As standalone GRTs** (bucket-A economics without the max() structure): Ohio
  CAT 0.26%, Texas Margin Tax 0.331%–0.75% (hybrid deduction-menu base), Washington
  B&O (35 rates, 0.13%–3.3%), Nevada Commerce (27 rates), Delaware (54 rates).
- **Distortion evidence**: Ohio example — a 1%-margin firm under a 0.26% GRT bears
  a **26% effective rate on net income** (the margin-floor arithmetic empirically);
  a 1% GRT costs a 5%-margin firm 20% of profits vs 2% for a 50%-margin firm ✓.
  **Pyramiding**: WA B&O cascades 2.5× on average (1.4× services to 6.7×
  manufacturing), producing effective rates on value added from <1% (retail) to
  >3% (utilities), and rewards tax-motivated vertical integration (integrating a
  four-stage lumber chain halves the effective rate) ✓; consistent with Thuronyi's
  merger worked example ✓✓ (3-0 twice).
- **Litigation**: Texas — Nestlé USA (2012) upheld differential rate
  classification; Titan Transportation (2014) and Hallmark Marketing were
  base-computation disputes ✓. Depression-era GRTs (15 states by 1934) were mostly
  repealed or struck down by WWII ✓.
- **Note**: a claim that the *2007-era* trend was toward GRT revival also appears
  in the pool ✓ — the two trajectories (revival, then renewed repeal) are both
  real, sequenced.

### 2.9 Cross-country evidence — IMF WP/21/161 (Aslam & Coelho), all ✓✓

The strongest verified block in the whole exercise (every claim 3-0):

1. **Prevalence**: 63 countries operated a corporate MT at end-2018; **31 on
   turnover** (10 assets, 9 modified income) — bucket A is the dominant form.
2. **Rate envelope**: turnover MT rates 0.1–3% (0.2% Tunisia to 3% Bolivia,
   Eq. Guinea, Guinea, Madagascar), average 1.2%; margin-floor equivalence stated
   exactly as in §2 of the brief.
3. **Effectiveness ranking**: event-study on firm panels — modified-income MTs
   raise average ETRs ~2.7 pp; assets ~1.2 pp; **turnover ~1.0 pp** (least per
   event, but see next). Slovakia's turnover-threshold "tax license": +1.5% ETR
   above the €500k threshold.
4. **Revenue**: only turnover-MT countries have out-collected non-MT countries in
   CIT since 2000 (up to ~1 pp of GDP more); a hypothetical 0.5% turnover MT yields
   a median ~0.2% of GDP (vs 0.9% for a 1% assets MT); Honduras 2016 ≈ +1% of GDP.
5. **Design synthesis**: turnover MTs are **erosion-resistant** (floor unaffected
   by deduction-based profit shifting; evadable only by under-reporting sales) but
   **margin-blind and cascading** — so the rate should proxy the CIT burden of a
   firm at the *lower end* of the margin distribution; **Romania** repealed its 5%
   turnover MT except for gambling/nightclubs.
6. **Creditability prescription** (✓✓ 4-0 with Thuronyi): MT payments creditable
   against future CIT with long carry-forwards; and draft the interaction so that
   **income-tax payments credit against the minimum tax** rather than
   "pay the greater," because the US allows no foreign tax credit for the excess of
   a minimum over the income tax (Rev. Rul. 91-45) — Mexico's IMPAC handled this
   with inflation-adjusted carry-forward, no carry-back.

### 2.10 Design theory — Thuronyi (IMF, Tax Law Design & Drafting, Ch. 12), all ✓✓

- Cascading/integration distortion: the 5%-floor worked example (two 4%-margin
  firms pay the presumption; merged, they escape it) — liability depends on
  integration, not income (3-0, twice).
- Margin heterogeneity: a single turnover rate is "highly inaccurate" as an income
  proxy; remedies are industry-specific margin schedules or a **rebuttable
  presumption** (Sierra Leone §23(3)) — but rebuttability "takes most of the teeth
  out" of the minimum. **Escape-valve generosity and enforcement bite trade off
  directly** (2-0/3-0).
- Regional bucket-B history: Argentina, Colombia, Mexico (2%), Venezuela (1%)
  asset minimums; Mexico's SCJN (22 Feb 1996) struck the financial-institution
  exemption from IMPAC ✓.

### 2.11 Not analogous / boundary cases (bucket C & D — kept short per brief §8)

- **Italy**: IRAP (3.9% ± 0.92 pp regional) is a value-added-type levy — bucket C,
  not A ✓. The società di comodo deemed-income regime could not be verified from
  the fetched pages (PwC summary omits it); statutory source is Law 724/1994 —
  **left as unknown rather than guessed**, per brief §5. Pillar Two via
  D.Lgs. 209/2023 is bucket D ✓.
- **India** MAT: 15% + surcharges on adjusted book profits — bucket D, not
  analogous ✓ (two independent extractions).
- **Bucket D generally** (Pillar Two, US CAMT): excluded per brief §3; mentioned
  only for contrast.
- **Presumptive small-firm turnover regimes are a different instrument**: IMF
  WP/23/267 (Wei & Wen) states explicitly that a turnover-based *alternative
  minimum* (the Best-et-al./Mexican structure) addresses CIT evasion and does not
  economize on compliance costs, and is "a different policy issue" from
  presumptive SME taxation (✓✓ 3-0 twice). The panel **killed (0-3)** a claim that
  Wei & Wen's optimal 2.5% presumptive rate is "directly comparable" to Mexico's
  2% minimum — the 2.5% optimum is for a *replacement* regime below a
  ~$65–95k threshold, not a floor on large-firm CIT. Their genuinely transferable
  findings (✓✓): the ETR implied by an optimal turnover rate may sit *below* the
  statutory CIT rate, contra the usual recommendation; and (✓, Wen How-To Note
  23/02) where a turnover minimum coexists with a presumptive SME regime, the SME
  turnover rate should exceed the minimum-tax turnover rate so graduation into the
  CIT regime is not discouraged — directly relevant to RESICO coexistence.
  Senegal's CGU (2% goods / 5% services below 50M XOF) and Tajikistan's 4% opt-in
  regime (<40% uptake in retail/construction — rate too high for low margins) are
  presumptive-regime datapoints on sector differentiation ✓.

## 3. Synthesis for the RF instrument (§6 questions)

### 3.1 Margin heterogeneity

The 6.67% floor implied by 2%/30% would be **the highest margin floor among
documented working bucket-A minimums** — above Honduras (6.0%), Panama (4.67%),
and far above Pakistan (1.43–2.86%); the global average turnover-MT rate is 1.2%
and the observed maximum 3% (✓✓). The margin-blindness pathology is quantified on
both continents: a 1%-margin firm under Ohio's 0.26% GRT pays 26% of its net
income; Washington's pyramiding varies 1.4–6.7× by industry. Every surviving
design handles heterogeneity somehow: Panama by **petition with deemed approval**
(the cleanest), Honduras by **halved sectoral rates + startup grace + loss
exemption**, Pakistan by **reduced sectoral rates**, Romania by **keeping the tax
only in high-evasion sectors**. Thuronyi's warning bounds the design space:
a fully rebuttable presumption removes the instrument's teeth — the valve must be
narrow (loss years, ETR-cap petitions) rather than a general low-margin opt-out.

### 3.2 Creditable vs. definitive

The evidence is sobering for the "creditability makes it a timing device" premise:
Pakistan's credit is claimed by **1.3% of firms** — creditable in form, definitive
in practice — and France's IFA drifted creditable → deductible → repealed. What the
✓✓ prescriptions actually support: long carry-forward periods, inflation
adjustment (Mexico's own IMPAC precedent), and drafting the credit as *CIT credits
against the minimum* rather than pay-the-greater, to preserve foreign tax credits
for inbound investors (Rev. Rul. 91-45 risk). If the RF instrument's creditability
is to be real rather than cosmetic, the binding question is *why* firms fail to
claim credits (chronic bindingness, compliance cost, expiry) — Pakistan suggests
chronically binding minimums simply become the tax.

### 3.3 Erosion resistance

Turnover minimums are the **only MT base whose adopters out-collected non-adopters
since 2000** (✓✓), because the base is immune to deduction manipulation — the
Honduran record shows exactly the cost-inflation channel (13–17% of profits) being
shut down. But the margin of evasion **migrates to revenue under-reporting**
(elasticity 0.35–1.0) and to **threshold bunching**, and the migration is
disciplined by third-party information — the single strongest empirical argument
that Mexico's CFDI environment is favorable terrain for this instrument. The
politically documented erosion channels are: threshold inflation (Honduras
L10M→L600M), rate/sector carve-outs (IMPAC's own history), and base narrowing.
US pyramiding evidence argues for keeping the instrument a *minimum* (as intended)
rather than letting it become a standalone GRT, and anticipating
vertical-integration responses in wholesale/retail chains.

### 3.4 Constitutionality

The map has two poles, and Mexico sits on the favorable one. Peru's strike-downs
(IMR 1996; AAIR 2004 — both **asset-based**, a correction to the brief's seed
list) rest on means–end incongruence and the constitutional relevance of loss
years for income-framed taxes; Guatemala (IEMA, 2003) and Costa Rica (2000, with
forced refunds) show regional courts will kill minimums on ability-to-pay and
double-taxation grounds. On the other pole, Mexico's SCJN (IETU, Feb 2010)
accepted **gross income as a legitimate manifestation of contributive capacity**
and blessed structural non-deductibility; Honduras's 2014 minimum was upheld in
2015 (after a 2011 version was struck). The design lessons that recur in what
survived: (i) standalone or clearly framed levy rather than a fiction inside the
ISR; (ii) creditability; (iii) an explicit valve for loss-making/low-margin firms
— the three features the Peruvian court itself cited when upholding the IEAN.

### 3.5 Closest working models

1. **Honduras (2014–2018)** — the same max() structure, a 6% floor adjacent to
   Mexico's 6.67%, upheld in court, with the best micro-evidence of both the gains
   (revenue +~1% GDP from newly liable firms; cost-inflation shut down) and the
   failure mode (threshold politics, not litigation, killed it).
2. **Pakistan (1991– )** — proof of durability and of the evasion-reduction
   mechanism at scale, and the cautionary result that a "minimum" with a low floor
   and weak credit uptake becomes the de facto corporate tax.
3. **Panama's CAIR** — the escape-valve architecture (loss/ETR-cap petition,
   deemed approval) that directly answers the margin-heterogeneity and
   constitutional objections simultaneously.

**Features correlating with success**: broad base + narrow, rule-based valves;
real creditability with long carry-forward; revenue third-party verification
(CFDI); rate calibrated to low-margin-firm CIT burden (i.e., a floor well below
the economy-wide average margin). **Features correlating with failure/repeal**:
rates implying implausible floors (Romania's 31%), lump-sum or bracket designs
(France), threshold-based exemptions large enough to invite political inflation
(Honduras), definitive (non-creditable) drift, and income-tax framing without
loss-year relief (Peru, Guatemala, Costa Rica).
